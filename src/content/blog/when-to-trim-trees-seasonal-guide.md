Pruning a tree at the wrong time of year can do more harm than good. In South Texas, timing matters even more than in cooler climates — our mild winters mean trees stay active longer, our pest pressures are different, and one deadly disease makes certain months off-limits for our most beloved species.

This guide covers the best time to prune the most common trees in the Kingsville and Coastal Bend area, along with signs that your trees need attention and when to call a professional.

---

## Why Timing Matters

When you cut a branch, you create an open wound. The tree responds by compartmentalizing — sealing off the wound to prevent infection and decay. How quickly and effectively a tree can do this depends largely on when the cut is made.

**Pruning at the right time:**

- Allows the tree to seal wounds before disease-carrying insects become active
- Minimizes stress by working with the tree's natural growth cycle
- Promotes healthy new growth in the right direction
- Reduces sap flow and bleeding at cut sites

**Pruning at the wrong time:**

- Exposes the tree to fungal infections and insect attack
- Can trigger weak, leggy growth that is vulnerable to storm damage
- May remove developing flower buds, costing you a season of blooms or fruit
- In the case of live oaks, can spread a lethal disease

> A bad pruning cut heals slowly. A bad pruning cut at the wrong time of year may not heal at all.

## Season-by-Season Guide for South Texas Trees

### Live Oaks — February Only

**Live oaks (*Quercus virginiana*)** are the signature tree of South Texas. They are also vulnerable to **oak wilt**, a devastating fungal disease spread by nitidulid beetles that are attracted to fresh pruning wounds.

> **Caution:** Never prune live oaks from March through June. This is peak oak wilt transmission season in Texas. The nitidulid beetles that spread the fungus are most active during these months, and a single fresh pruning wound can infect and kill a mature tree.

**The safe pruning window for live oaks in our area is February.** During this month, beetle activity is at its lowest, and the oak wilt fungus is least active. Here is the protocol:

- **Prune only in February** whenever possible
- **Paint all cuts immediately** with wound sealant or latex paint — this is the one exception where wound sealer is recommended, because it prevents beetle access to fresh wood
- **Sterilize tools** between trees with a **10% bleach solution** or Lysol spray to prevent cross-contamination
- **Never prune live oaks from March through June** — this is peak oak wilt transmission season in Texas

If a live oak has storm damage or a hazardous limb outside of February, remove the hazard immediately but seal the wound within minutes, not hours. Then schedule follow-up structural pruning for the next February window.

Oak wilt has been confirmed in multiple Texas counties and continues to spread. This is not an area where timing is flexible — it is critical.

### Mesquite — Late Winter to Early Spring

**Mesquite trees (*Prosopis glandulosa*)** are incredibly tough, but they respond best to pruning during their brief dormant period. In the Kingsville area, that window is **late January through early March**, before new leaf growth begins.

- Remove dead, crossing, or rubbing branches to improve structure
- Thin the canopy to reduce wind resistance — mesquites are prone to splitting in strong storms
- Avoid heavy pruning during summer, as it stimulates excessive water sprouts (suckers) that weaken the tree
- Mesquite thorns make this one of the more challenging trees to prune — heavy leather gloves and eye protection are essential

---

### Citrus Trees — After Last Frost (March)

South Texas is citrus country, and many Kingsville properties have **grapefruit, orange, Meyer lemon, satsuma, or lime trees**. The best time to prune citrus is **March**, after the last typical frost date (mid-February for our area) but before the spring growth flush.

- **Remove dead, damaged, or diseased wood** at any time of year
- **Shape and thin** in March to improve air circulation and light penetration into the canopy
- **Remove suckers and water sprouts** growing from below the graft union or from the trunk
- **Do not prune heavily in fall or winter** — it stimulates tender new growth that is vulnerable to frost damage
- **Skip pruning during active fruiting** unless removing clearly dead or diseased branches

> **Pro Tip:** Light pruning is usually all citrus needs. These trees naturally maintain a productive shape without aggressive cutting. If you can see through the canopy, you have pruned enough.

### Crape Myrtles — Late Winter (February to Early March)

Let us talk about **crape murder**. Every winter in South Texas, you see crape myrtles hacked back to bare trunks — thick branches cut to the same height, leaving ugly knobs that sprout weak, whip-like growth. This practice is unnecessary, harmful, and produces an inferior tree.

**The right way to prune crape myrtles:**

- **Timing:** February through early March, while the tree is still dormant
- **Remove only:** Suckers from the base, crossing or rubbing branches, dead wood, and seed pods from last season
- **Thin selectively** by removing smaller interior branches to open up the canopy — this improves air circulation and reduces powdery mildew
- **Never top the tree** by cutting major branches to a uniform height. Crape myrtles bloom on new wood, and they will flower prolifically whether you hack them or not
- **If the tree is too big for the space**, it was the wrong variety — consider replacing it with a dwarf or semi-dwarf cultivar rather than fighting its natural size every year

> **Caution:** Topping crape myrtles — "crape murder" — does not produce more blooms. It produces weak, whip-like branches that are prone to breaking, ugly knobbed trunks, and a tree that will never develop the graceful form crape myrtles are known for. Just stop.

Properly pruned crape myrtles have graceful, sculptural trunks with smooth bark and an open, vase-shaped canopy. They bloom more heavily and live longer than those subjected to annual topping.

### Palm Trees — Late Spring (May to June)

**Texas Sabal palms, Mexican fan palms, and Washingtonia palms** are common in Kingsville landscapes. Palm pruning is straightforward but has specific rules:

- **Wait until late spring** when any risk of cold damage has passed and you can clearly see which fronds are dead
- **Only remove completely brown, dead fronds** — never cut green or yellow fronds, as these are still feeding the tree
- **Follow the "9 and 3" rule** — imagine the palm crown as a clock face. Never remove fronds above the 9 o'clock to 3 o'clock line (horizontal). Over-trimming, sometimes called "hurricane cutting," actually makes palms **more** vulnerable to storm damage, not less
- **Remove fruit clusters** if desired, as they attract pests and create a mess
- **Do not use climbing spikes** — the puncture wounds invite disease and permanently scar the trunk

---

### Huisache and Texas Ebony — Late Winter

These native South Texas trees are best pruned in **late January through February** during dormancy:

- **Huisache** (*Vachellia farnesiana*) — Prune to maintain a single trunk if desired and remove low-hanging thorny branches near walkways. The fragrant yellow blooms appear in late winter on old wood, so prune immediately after flowering if preserving blooms is a priority.
- **Texas Ebony** (*Ebenopsis ebano*) — A slow grower that rarely needs heavy pruning. Remove dead wood and shape lightly during dormancy. This tree is too valuable and slow-growing to prune aggressively.

## Signs Your Tree Needs Attention

Beyond seasonal maintenance pruning, watch for these warning signs year-round:

- **Dead or hanging branches** — A safety hazard that should be addressed promptly regardless of season
- **Crossing or rubbing limbs** — These create wounds where bark is worn away, inviting disease
- **Dense canopy with poor airflow** — Promotes fungal disease and provides cover for pests
- **Leaning or unbalanced growth** — May indicate root problems or structural weakness
- **Cracks in major limbs or the trunk** — A sign of structural failure that needs professional assessment
- **Fungal growth (mushrooms or conks) on the trunk or major roots** — Indicates internal decay
- **Excessive deadwood throughout the canopy** — May signal decline from disease, drought stress, or root damage

After severe storms — and we get our share in the Coastal Bend — inspect your trees for cracked limbs, hanging branches, and uprooted root systems. Storm damage should be addressed as soon as it is safe to do so.

## DIY Pruning vs. Professional Tree Work

**Handle it yourself if:**

- Branches are smaller than **3 inches** in diameter
- You can reach them safely from the ground with a pole pruner or hand saw
- The work is limited to removing dead wood, suckers, or small crossing branches
- No power lines or structures are within falling distance

**Call a professional if:**

- Branches are larger than **4 inches** in diameter or high in the canopy
- The tree is near power lines, your home, or other structures
- You need to climb the tree or use a ladder (ladder falls are a leading cause of homeowner injuries)
- The tree shows signs of disease or structural failure
- You are dealing with a live oak during any month other than February
- Storm damage has left large, hanging limbs

> **Pro Tip:** Always make pruning cuts just outside the **branch collar** — the slightly swollen area where the branch meets the trunk. Never leave long stubs, and never make flush cuts against the trunk. A proper collar cut lets the tree compartmentalize the wound efficiently and heal cleanly.

**Safety equipment for DIY pruning:**

- Sharp bypass pruners, loppers, and a hand saw (never use a chainsaw from a ladder)
- Safety glasses and heavy gloves
- Hard hat if working under the canopy
- Long sleeves — especially around thorny species like mesquite and huisache
