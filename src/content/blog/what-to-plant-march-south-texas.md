## Vegetables to Plant in March in South Texas

If you have been waiting to start your spring vegetable garden, the wait is over. Zone 9b soil temperatures are warm enough by early March for most warm-season crops, and our average last frost date falls in mid-February, so the risk has passed.

### Warm-Season Vegetables Ready to Go in the Ground

- **Tomatoes** — Plant transplants now for a harvest before the worst of summer. Varieties like Celebrity, Roma, and Cherry do well here. Get them in early so they can fruit before nighttime temperatures stay above **75 degrees** and blossoms start dropping.
- **Peppers** — Jalapenos, serranos, bell peppers, and banana peppers all thrive in our climate. Transplants will establish faster than seeds at this stage.
- **Squash and zucchini** — Direct sow seeds in the garden. These grow fast in South Texas warmth and can produce within **50 days**.
- **Bush beans and pole beans** — Plant seeds directly. Beans fix nitrogen in the soil, so they benefit everything around them.
- **Cucumbers** — Direct sow or transplant. Give them a trellis — they produce better with airflow off the ground.
- **Eggplant** — Another heat-lover that does well in our long growing season. Plant transplants for a head start.

> **Pro Tip:** Get your tomatoes in the ground by early March. They need time to set fruit before nighttime temperatures stay above 75 degrees, which causes blossom drop. Every week you wait in March is a week less of harvest.

### Herbs to Start Now

- **Basil** — Wait until nighttime lows stay above **50 degrees** consistently, which is usually mid-March here. Basil hates cold feet.
- **Cilantro** — Plant it now before it gets too hot. Cilantro bolts quickly once temperatures hit the 80s, so get it in early March for the longest harvest window.
- **Rosemary, oregano, and thyme** — These Mediterranean herbs love our hot, dry conditions. Plant once and enjoy them for years.
- **Mint** — Plant in a container unless you want it everywhere. Mint is vigorous in our climate.

> **Caution:** Always plant mint in a container. In South Texas, mint spreads aggressively by runners and will take over garden beds, choking out neighboring plants within a single growing season.

## Flowers That Thrive When Planted in March

March is also prime time for adding color to your landscape. The flowers that do well here are tough, heat-tolerant, and many are pollinator magnets.

- **Zinnias** — Direct sow seeds after the last frost. Zinnias love full sun and our hot summers. They bloom continuously and attract butterflies all season.
- **Marigolds** — Tough, cheerful, and practically indestructible in South Texas. They also help deter certain garden pests near your vegetables.
- **Lantana** — A Coastal Bend staple. Lantana handles drought, poor soil, and full sun without complaint. Butterflies cannot resist it.
- **Salvias** — Both annual and perennial salvias perform well here. Try autumn sage (*Salvia greggii*) for a native option that blooms spring through fall and attracts hummingbirds.
- **Pentas** — One of the top butterfly and hummingbird plants for South Texas. Plant in full sun and enjoy nonstop blooms until the first frost.
- **Moss rose (Portulaca)** — Perfect for hot, dry spots where other flowers give up. Blooms in brilliant colors all summer with almost no water.

## Trees and Shrubs to Plant in March

Spring is an excellent time to add trees and shrubs to your landscape. Plants installed now have months to develop root systems before summer stress.

> The best time to plant a tree was twenty years ago. The second best time is this March.

**Trees worth planting now:**

- **Desert Willow** (*Chilopsis linearis*) — Drought-tolerant, fast-growing, with showy tubular flowers that hummingbirds love.
- **Texas Ebony** (*Ebenopsis ebano*) — A beautiful native evergreen that handles our heat and alkaline soil.
- **Mexican Plum** (*Prunus mexicana*) — Already finished blooming by March, but still an excellent time to plant. Provides spring flowers, summer shade, and fruit for wildlife.
- **Live Oak** (*Quercus virginiana*) — The king of South Texas shade trees. Plant now and give it room to spread.

**Shrubs to consider:**

- **Texas Sage** (*Leucophyllum frutescens*) — Blooms purple after summer rains. Virtually no maintenance once established.
- **Esperanza** (*Tecoma stans*) — Bright yellow trumpet flowers from spring through fall. Hummingbird favorite.
- **Turk's Cap** (*Malvaviscus arboreus*) — Shade-tolerant native with red blooms that hummingbirds and butterflies seek out.

## Spring Planting Tips for South Texas

Getting your plants in the ground is only half the battle. These tips help them establish and thrive in our specific conditions.

### Prepare Your Soil

South Texas soils tend to be heavy clay or sandy caliche. Before planting, work in **2 to 3 inches of compost** to improve drainage and add organic matter. Do not amend too deeply for trees — you want roots to grow outward into native soil, not stay in a cushy pocket.

### Water Smart from Day One

New plantings need consistent moisture to establish roots, but overwatering in our heavy soils causes root rot. Water deeply and less frequently rather than a little every day. A good rule of thumb:

- **Vegetables and annuals** — Water every 2 to 3 days until established, then adjust based on rainfall.
- **Trees and shrubs** — Deep soak at planting, then once or twice a week for the first growing season.
- **Consider drip irrigation** — It delivers water directly to roots with minimal waste. **Need a hands-off setup?** [Our irrigation team](/services/irrigation/) can design and install the perfect system for your property.

> **Pro Tip:** Water deeply and less frequently rather than giving plants a light sprinkle every day. Deep watering encourages roots to grow downward, making plants far more resilient when summer heat and drought arrive.

### Mulch Generously

Apply **3 to 4 inches of mulch** around plants (but keep it a few inches away from stems and trunks). Mulch keeps soil cooler, retains moisture, and suppresses weeds — all critical in our climate.

### Watch for Late Cold Snaps

While rare, South Texas can get a surprise cold front into mid-March. Keep frost cloth or old sheets handy for tender transplants like tomatoes, peppers, and basil during the first few weeks.
