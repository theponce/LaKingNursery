## Understanding What South Texas Summer Does to Your Lawn

Summer in USDA Zone 9b is not just hot — it is relentlessly hot. Kingsville averages over **100 days above 90 degrees**, with soil temperatures that can reach **130 degrees** at the surface. That kind of sustained heat stresses even warm-season grasses, causing browning, thinning, and vulnerability to pests and disease.

The key challenges your lawn faces include:

- **Triple-digit air temperatures** that push grass into survival mode
- **Intense UV exposure** with **14+ hours** of summer daylight
- **Drought conditions** that can stretch for weeks between rain events
- **Hot, drying winds** off the Gulf that increase evaporation
- **Compacted clay soils** common throughout Kleberg County that hold heat and repel water

> The window for summer lawn prep in South Texas is short. By the time you feel the heat, it is too late for most of these steps.

Starting your prep early — ideally by late February — gives your lawn time to build deep roots and strong blades before the heat arrives.

## Choosing the Right Grass for South Texas

Not every grass can handle what our summers dish out. Here are the **warm-season grasses** that perform well in the Coastal Bend:

### St. Augustine (*Stenotaphrum secundatum*)

The most popular lawn grass in South Texas for good reason. **Floratam** and **Palmetto** varieties handle our heat, tolerate partial shade, and produce a thick, lush carpet. St. Augustine needs more water than other options — roughly **1 inch per week** during summer — but rewards you with that classic green lawn look.

### Bermuda Grass (*Cynodon dactylon*)

If your yard gets full sun, Bermuda is tough to beat. It is the most drought-tolerant and heat-resistant option available, recovers quickly from damage, and stands up to heavy foot traffic. **Celebration** and **TifTuf** varieties are excellent choices for the Kingsville area. Bermuda needs about **0.75 to 1 inch** of water per week.

### Zoysia (*Zoysia japonica*)

A good middle ground between St. Augustine and Bermuda. Zoysia tolerates moderate shade, feels soft underfoot, and requires less water than St. Augustine — about **0.75 inch per week**. The downside is slower establishment and recovery. **Palisades** and **Zeon** are well-suited varieties for our zone.

### Buffalo Grass (*Buchloe dactyloides*)

The lowest-maintenance option for large properties or areas where you want a natural prairie look. Buffalo grass is native to Texas and survives on rainfall alone in most years. It goes dormant and turns golden in drought but greens up quickly after rain. Ideal for rural properties around Kingsville that do not need a manicured look.

## Spring Prep Timeline: February Through April

A strong summer lawn starts with deliberate spring preparation. Here is a month-by-month plan for the Coastal Bend:

### February: Wake Up and Clean Up

- **Rake out dead material and debris** that accumulated over winter
- **Test your soil** — our alkaline, clay-heavy soils often need amendments (sulfur to lower pH, gypsum to improve drainage)
- **Check your irrigation system** for leaks, clogged heads, and coverage gaps before you need it daily. **Spot problems early?** [Our irrigation team can help](/services/irrigation/).
- **Apply a pre-emergent herbicide** before soil temperatures consistently hit **55 degrees** to prevent crabgrass and other summer annuals

### March: First Feeding and First Mow

- **Apply a slow-release fertilizer** with a **3-1-2 ratio** (such as 15-5-10) once nighttime temperatures stay above **60 degrees** — typically mid-March in Kingsville
- **Mow for the first time** once grass is actively growing, setting your mower at the upper height range for your grass type
- **Address bare patches** by laying sod or plugs while temperatures are still mild and rain is more frequent

### April: Strengthen and Protect

- **Aerate compacted areas** to improve water and nutrient penetration into our heavy clay soils
- **Apply a second round of pre-emergent** if you did not use an extended-release product in February
- **Adjust your irrigation schedule** upward as temperatures climb and rainfall decreases
- **Scout for early pest activity**, especially chinch bugs in St. Augustine lawns

> **Pro Tip:** Apply pre-emergent herbicide when soil temperatures reach 55 degrees — not when you see weeds. Once you can see crabgrass, it is too late for pre-emergent to work. A soil thermometer is a worthwhile investment.

## Mowing Height: Higher Than You Think

One of the most common mistakes we see in South Texas is mowing too short. Taller grass shades the soil, retains moisture, and develops deeper roots. Here are the recommended mowing heights for summer:

| Grass Type | Mowing Height | Water Needs/Week |
|---|---|---|
| **St. Augustine** | 3.5 to 4 inches | ~1 inch |
| **Bermuda** | 1.5 to 2 inches | 0.75–1 inch |
| **Zoysia** | 2 to 2.5 inches | ~0.75 inch |
| **Buffalo Grass** | 3 to 4 inches (or natural) | Rainfall only |

> **Caution:** Never remove more than one-third of the blade length in a single mow. Cutting too short — called "scalping" — exposes the soil to direct sun, drives up soil temperature, and forces the grass to spend energy regrowing blades instead of deepening roots. In a South Texas summer, scalped grass may not recover.

During peak summer, that one-third rule may mean mowing every **5 to 7 days**. Keep your blades sharp — ragged cuts stress the grass and invite disease.

## Watering Smart, Not Just Watering More

In South Texas, efficient watering is everything. Here is how to get the most from every drop:

- **Water deeply and infrequently** — **0.5 to 0.75 inch** per session, two to three times per week, rather than light daily sprinkles
- **Water early in the morning** (before 8 AM) to minimize evaporation and give blades time to dry before nightfall
- **Use the tuna can test** — set empty cans around your yard while sprinklers run to measure actual output and identify coverage gaps
- **Watch for footprinting** — if your grass does not spring back after you walk on it, it needs water now

> **Pro Tip:** Set three or four empty tuna cans around your yard and run your sprinklers for 15 minutes. Measure the water in each can. This tells you exactly how much water your system puts out — and, more importantly, where the coverage gaps are.

A properly designed [irrigation system](/services/irrigation/) can reduce water waste significantly. Drip zones for beds and matched-precipitation rotary heads for turf areas make a real difference on your water bill.

## Fertilization Schedule for the Growing Season

South Texas lawns benefit from regular feeding during the active growing months. Here is a straightforward schedule:

1. **Mid-March:** Slow-release nitrogen fertilizer (15-5-10 or similar) at the rate listed on the bag
2. **Late May:** Second application of slow-release nitrogen
3. **September:** Final feeding to help grass recover and store energy before winter dormancy

> **Caution:** Avoid fertilizing during the hottest weeks of July and August. Fertilizer forces top growth when the plant needs to focus on root survival, which can actually push stressed grass over the edge. And never apply fertilizer to dry, stressed grass — water thoroughly the day before.

## Dealing With Summer Pests and Weeds

### Chinch Bugs

The number one lawn pest in South Texas St. Augustine lawns. Look for irregular yellow patches that start at the edges of sunny areas and expand outward. Part the grass and look for tiny black-and-white insects near the soil line. Treat with bifenthrin or a granular insecticide at the first sign of damage.

### Grub Worms

White, C-shaped larvae that feed on grass roots, causing spongy turf that pulls up easily. Apply a preventive grub treatment (containing chlorantraniliprole) in **May or June** before damage appears.

### Crabgrass and Sandbur

Both are summer annual weeds that thrive in our heat. The best defense is a thick, healthy lawn and timely pre-emergent application in February. If they break through, spot-treat with a post-emergent labeled for your grass type — be careful, as some herbicides damage St. Augustine.

## When to Call in the Pros

DIY lawn care can take you a long way, but some situations call for professional help:

- **Persistent brown patches** that do not respond to watering or fertilization
- **Severe pest infestations** that are spreading faster than you can treat them
- **Irrigation problems** like uneven coverage, high water bills, or system malfunctions
- **Major renovation** — if more than **40%** of your lawn needs replacement, professional sodding saves time and delivers better results
- **Soil issues** like severe compaction, drainage problems, or extreme pH imbalance
